GAMEPlAN is an active music curriculum for children. Organized by grade level and 
presented chronologically in weekly lessons, GAMEPlAN sequences musical skil ls and 
objectives that support the National Standards for Arts Education. As good teaching is 
diverse, this curriculum is eclectic. It embraces several philosophies, including the 
principles of Orff Schulwerk and the Kodaly approach to reading rhythm and melody. Folk 
songs, folk rhymes, and traditional games and dances form an integral part of the repertoire 
included. 
GAMEPlAN has two goals: 
• to organize musical objectives in a logical progression that is educationally 
sound and developmentally appropriate 
• to present the lessons in a concise format that is practical, easy to follow, and 
user-friendly 
GAMEPlAN parallels the school year from September through May and includes one 
lesson per week with approximately 60 minutes of activities. This is an active music 
curriculum. There are no student textbooks. All charts and visuals appear in the 
Appendices, and any additional materials required are listed on page x. Included in Grade 
Four are activities from folk dance collections and the first volume of Carl Orff and Gunild 
Keetman's Music for Children. These resources will also be referenced in Grade Five. 
New to Grade Four is Cool TW1es, a set of 22 reproducible pages for students (located 
inside the pocket of the back cover). Cool TW1eS introduces the soprano recorder with 
songs and activity pages integrated throughout the school year. Permission is granted to 
dupl icate these pages annually for students in one school. These pages may not be shared 
with other teachers as this is an infringement of copyright. 
Two new Appendices have been added to this grade level ... Appendix 0: Recorder and 
Appendix E: Performing Croups. Each contains useful information. 
The followi ng pages define and outline Grade Four of this curriculum. Take time to 
familiarize yourself with this information, as it explains how and why the lessons unfold. 
GAMEPLAN is the result of a combined total of over 50 years of teaching music to 
ch ildren. During that time many teachers, colleagues, friends, and family influenced our 
work; however, the greatest impact on this project came from our students. For all of the 
joy, fascination, frustration, and wonder they provide, GAMEPlAN is dedicated to those 
children ... our greatest teachers.

Shadowing 
As an extension to mirroring, shadowing is exactly what it implies: the action 
duplicates the movement from behind, and the same sides of the body are 
used. 
In standing scattered formation, students and teacher (leader) face the same 
direction. Teacher leads slow, fluid movements as students copy. Incorporate 
different levels and shapes and isolate sides of the body. 
Identify a student leader and play the fol lowing recording. Encourage the 
leader to let the music inspire the movement.

combinations of pulses as this requires the students to differentiate between the 
high and low registers of the piano.

a hand drum. Whi le walking, students whisper, 110 nc, two, three, four, one,

Shadowing in Groups 
Introduced in November (page 38), shadowi ng is exactly what it implies: the 
action duplicates the movement from behind, and the same sides of the body 
are used. 
Students work in groups of four standing at the points of a diamond all facing 
the same direction. The student at the forward point (north) of the diamond is 
the leader, and the other three students shadow the leader. When the leader 
chooses lo pass the lead, s/he may turn to the right (cast) or the left (west) and 
all four students pivot in that direction designating a new leader. 
Recording: 
Encourage students to pass the lead in 
silence and to reflect the tempo and 
mood of the music in their movement.

Most elementary music programs include at least one performing group, and the possibilities 
are many. Although the most obvious is an elementary choir, ensembles may also be 
instrumental or movement based. Regardless of the type of ensemble, it is wise to wait until 
students are in the upper grades bciore providing them this opportunity. Potentially large 
numbers, limited rehearsal time, and the sophistication of performance repertoire make it 
more appropriate for older students. 
Consider including both fourth and fiith grade students in ensembles, as this will build the 
prograrn from year to year, and the older students can model musicianship and desired 
behaviors. 
Often the new music specialist inherits cin existing performance group. If this is not the case, 
the teacher may choose from a variety of options, such as:

A difficult but important decision ... Is the perform ing group auditioned or open to every 
student? Although no student should be turned away, the most musically satisfying 
experiences will be generated from students that meet certain criteria. One possible solution 
is to have more than one ensemble. For example, students that don't match pitch may be 
an asset to an instrumental group, and a student that has difficulty functioning in a large 
ensemble may be better placed in a smaller group. 
Even if all students are welcome to participate, it is good ior them to experience the audition 
process. Choose and announce a specific time/s (before/after school, lunch recess or during 
class), and have students complete a short survey listing name, grade, classroom teacher, 
and which performing group is of interest. Consider the following suggestions: 
Choir auditions 
• teach all students part oi a new song (with a range of at least an octave) 
• listen to students in small groups or duets and then individually 
Instrument auditions 
• echo clap eight-beat phrases with the whole group and then individually 
• teach a short pattern to be played on barred instruments, drums, or recorder; 
students play in small groups and then individual!)' 
Movement auditions 
• echo eight-beat phrases with four levels of body percussion first with the 
whole group and then individually 
• teach a short movement sequence (include specific right/left and balance 
challenges); students perform in small groups When auditioning Jny group: 
• mark each survey cJrd with a plus or minus 
• note cJny behavior issues (a student that has difficulty during the audition 
process will likely have problems in rehearsals) 
• do not announce results until all students have auditioned 
Finally, think about the optimum size for each performing group. This may be influenced 
by the size of the rehearsal/performance spaces or the number of instruments available. 
Rehearsals 
Don'! waste time. The most effective rehearsals are well planned and follow a routine'. 
Rehearse the choir in the performance formation. Choirs should stretch, breathe, and 
vocalize before rehearsing specific repertoire. Student officers can take roll and monitor 
behavior while the choir is "warming up." All performing groups should come to rehearsals 
prepared (choir folders, recorders, etc.). Lisi the rehearsal agenda on the board in order 
lo stay focused crncl on time. Verbalize expected beha\ iors and con equences, and be 
consistent. Develop an attendance policy and adhere to it. 
Communication 
Communication is key and begins at the top. Each aspect of the performing group 
(rehearsal times, expectations, policies, etc.) needs to be approved by the administrator. 
Classroom teachers appreciate as much notice as possible when planning final rehearsals 
and performances. Parents need to understand in writing V\httt is expected of their child 
and should sign t:1 letter of agreement. Monthly memos are helpful in reiterating rehearsal 
schedules, performC1ncc dates, uncl deadlines.
