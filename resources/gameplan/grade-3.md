Objectives are categorized into five conceptual areas. 
Rhythm - demonstrate pulse (quarter, half, dotted half, and whole note), recognize 
simple note values, identify time signatures, and begin to develop 
Question-Answer improvisation 
Melody - demonstrate proper vocal technique, explore Major and minor pentatonic 
melodies that support movable Do, identify treble clef lines and 
spaces, and continue to build repertoire 
Harmony-develop mallet technique, explore ostinato, distinguish between Major 
and minor, and experience canon/round 
Form - identify and label simple forms and experience extended forms 
Timbre - continue to identify musical symbols and label the orchestral string and 
orchestral percussion families 
Students approach objectives through a variety of experiences. 
Move - explore locomotor and nonlocomotor responses in metered and 
non-metered settings and begin organized dance 
Speak-use the voice rhythmically or freely to explore vocal expression 
Sing - match pitch independently and within a group 
Listen - develop aural recognition 
Play - explore the body as an instrument as well as pitched and unpitched percussion 
Notate - demonstrate an understanding of the visual representation of rhythm, 
melody, and form 
Lessons unfold through different Stages of Learning. 
Imitation-teacher-modeled response, performed simultaneously or as an echo 
Exploration - the opportunity to alter a musical experience through experimentation 
Improvisation - creation of a musical response or idea with or without the element 
of time 
Visualization - the use of graphic or traditional notation leading to music literacy 
Objectives are presented through different Stages of Teaching. 
Introduce - presentation of objectives as an idea or a labeled experience 
Reinforce - repetition as an opportunity for practice 
Assess - evaluation through observation or written response

Third grade students have great curithity. They are more socially aware and cognizant of 
others' feelings. In the music classroom, they are ready for more group work and at times 
can work independently. Because they better understand consequences, they can be given 
more opportunities to make choices. 
GAMERAN includes one lesson per week with approximately 60 minutes of activities. 
Each of the lessons is planned sequentially, addressing objectives through a variety of 
experiences in different stages of learning. Many considerations were made when planning 
the lessons; however, most teachers do not instruct students once a week for an hour. 
Consequently, as you adapt the lessons to your own teaching circumstances, keep in mind 
the following: 
• although attention spans have increased, third grade students still need a variety 
of activities in each lesson (if you see students more than once a week, 
consider introducing the activities during the first lesson and reviewing or 
extending them during the second) 
• if you instruct students less than 60 minutes per week, consider the stage of 
teaching for each activity (introduce, reinforce, assess) when determining 
which ones to include 
• most teachers will not have all of the instruments and materials needed to teach 
every lesson, but do not be discouraged . . . building a music program is an 
ongoing process and requires flexibility and a willingness to adapt 
In every lesson: 
• state the objective(s) at the beginning and review the concepts at the end 
• transition from activity to activity quickly 
• develop ways to "re-focus" the students during an activity; for example: 
stand/sit, reposition your placement in the room, introduce a visual, etc. 
• include movement. .. in everylesson 
• develop questioning techniques that require higher-level thinking skills 
• see the experience through the students' eyes. . . What is special? What will 
they enjoy? What will they share with their parents when they get home? 
Unless you are a beginning teacher, you probably have activities you have successfully 
experienced with your third grade students. If you would like to incorporate them into 
GAMEPIAN: 
• identify which objective(s) the activity best supports (be certain the teaching 
process teaches to the objective) 
• determine whether the activity best introduces, reinforces, or assesses the 
objective (this will help you know when to teach the activity) 
• consult the "Year At A Glance" chart on page viii to know when to substitute 
or add the activity 
NATIONAL STANDARDS • MUSIC 
The National Standards for Arts Education - Music Content Standards: 
1. Singing, alone and with others, a varied repertoire of music 
2. Performing on instruments, alone and with others, a varied repertoire of music 
3. Improvising melodies, variations, and accompaniments 
4. Composing and arranging music within specified guidelines 
5. Reading and notating music 
6. Listening to, analyzing, and describing music 
7. Evaluating music and music performances 
8. Understanding relationships between music, the other arts, and disciplines outside the arts 
9. Understanding music in relation to history and culture Children learn by doing. They need room to move and should be active participants in the music making. To 
do so, the classroom should provide an open space for movement and lots of instruments. See Appendix F 
for a complete list of what the ideal music room should include.

Using up to four levels of body percussion (snap - clap - pat - stamp) reinforce 
the length of the phrase (four measures in 2/4) ending on the first beat of the 
fourth measure; for example:

This activity, introduced in Grade One and reviewed in Grade Two, has longterm goals: 
- to establish the length of the phrase 
- to improve rhythmic accuracy exploring four levels of 
body percussion 
- to instill a sense of meter 
- to prepare students for rhythmic improvisation

Visualizing Whole Note 
This short activity prepares whole note through visualization. 
Process: - choose four students to stand in a row facing the class 
- walking behind the row, tap the head of each student as

from left to right.

the teacher taps four heads

the first beat and turn the palm up to mark the 
second beat. 
- to prepare whole note, ask the second, third, and fourth 
students to squat; class discovers how to speak 
the pattern ("Ta -a-a-a")

Four Beats After 
Introduced at the end of Grade Two, Four Beats After is an easy way to expose 
students to the technique of canons and rounds. In a standing circle the 
teacher, as the leader, begins a sequence of actions representing the pulse 
that changes after every four beats. The class echoes the teacher beginning 
the sequence 'four beats after;' for example: 
1 - 
IJJJJ 
(clap) (pat) snap) stamp) 
Keep the sequence relatively short (5-6 measures), choosing actions or body 
percussion that contrast in levels. To end the activity, consider freezing into 
a shape such as arms folded across chest.

Note: This definition of canon/round will be reviewed 
next week, as it may be performed as a canon. 
Initially use the terms canon and round interchangeably. Young students do 
not need to know the distinctions between the two. Although similar, the 
imitating part of a canon usually follows at a shorter distance than that of a 
round. Melodic canons may be performed at different intervals (such as the 
fifth or the fourth), where rounds are always performed at the unison or 
octave. Canons may appear in augmentation or diminution, but each part of 
a round is equal in length. It is interesting to note that a round is a circle 
canon which is always repeated.

It is important to give students the opportunity to notate rhythmically. In small 
groups allow all students to draw four sixteenth notes on the board. Be

Stage of Learning: Improvisation 
Improvisation grows out of imitation; therefore students need lots of echo 
play before beginning the Question-Answer technique of rhythmic 
improvisation. GAMEPLAN carefully sequences this process beginning in 
Grade One and is designed to successfully prepare students for this 
technique. 
When approaching Question-Answer improvisation: 
• begin with 2/4 meter (four measures) 
• be certain the Question and the Answer are the same length 
• ensure phrases are seven beats in length, ending on the first beat 
of the fourth measure 
Note: Later the question will fill in the eighth beat. 
• begin with one or two levels of body percussion, gradually extending 
to four levels 
Note: Question-Answer improvisation will progress from 
body percussion to unpitched and pitched instruments 
in Grades Four and Five. 
• begin with simple rhythm patterns, later introducing syncopation, 
dotted rhythms, and off-beats 
Process: - demonstrate the concept of a conversation by asking 
a student a question; for example: 
"What's your favorite ice cream?" 
- encourage the student to answer in a complete sentence: 
"Chocolate is my favorite ice cream." 
- transfer this idea of equal length phrases to musical 
questions and answers beginning with a seven-beat 
question comprised of six quarter notes and two

Leprechauns, Wizards, and Giants 
This movement activity is a variation of the "Stone-Scissors-Paper" game, 
played with partners; however, in this version the partners change as students 
are eliminated. Students each choose a partner and stand back to back. On 
a given sound cue (teacher plays hand drum) partners turn to face one 
another assuming one of the following shapes and making the corresponding 
sound: 
If you choose Leprechaun, create a low 
statue, knees touching and elbows 
touching, and make a high pitched 
sound... "eee-eeeeee" 
If you choose Wizard, create a medium 
statue, shake palms while hands are 
parallel to the floor, and make a 
buzzing sound... "zzzzzzzzzzt" 
If you choose Giant, create a high statue, 
arms up, and "roar" 
• Leprechaun beats Wizard 
• Wizard beats Giant 
• Giant beats Leprechaun 
The student that is eliminated in each set of partners sits out while the winners 
find new partners. Game continues until there is one winner. Repeat game 
as time allows. 
Games like this build sportsmanship. 
It is important for students to learn 
they will not always win.

Review this activity (see page 93). The teacher asks the question using one 
or two levels of body percussion, and the class simultaneously gives the 
answer. Be certain the improvisation is in 2/4 and is eight beats in length, 
ending on beat seven (both the question and the answer).

either touching fingertips to mark the downbeat of each measure, moving 
hands closer to one another as the phrase comes to an end, or drawing the 
arc of the phrase. 
As students will benefit from lots of repetition over a period of time, keep this 
activity short. It will be repeated in subsequent lessons.

Shown below are the pentatonic scales available on the pitched percussion instruments. 
The most common pentatonic scales used in Orff Schulwerk are based on Do-Re-Mi-So-La; 
consequently there are no half steps. 
Each of the major pentatonic scales has a relative minor. The notes are the same, however 
the tonal center changes. Minor scales are based on La.

the relative minor (11 minor) is avoided. Few teachers begin a teaching assignment with adequate instruments and equipment. 
Furnishing a music room often requires the support of the administration and community. 
It can take several years. Fund raising, soliciting assistance from parent organizations, and 
applying for local or national grants should all be considered when equipping the music 
room. 
Use the information listed below as a guide. The size of the teaching space and the amount 
of students per class will impact the numbers. Ideally the music room should have a large 
open space for movement. Children do not mind sitting on the floor. If the average class 
size is between 25-30 students, the following is recommended: 
• pitched instruments

Buy quality instruments. Begin with a bass and alto 
xylophone and one each of the glockenspiels. Add 
multiples and metallophones when able. Eventually 
consider six to eight glockenspiels, as they are less 
expensive and afford more students the opportunity to 
play. If you are unable to purchase all of the contra 
bass bars at one time, begin with the C-D-F-G and 
add the others later. Students will demonstrate better 
mallet technique if instruments are raised off of the 
floor. Stands are available and are helpful when 
instruments need to be moved. 
• unpitched instruments 
Wood - wood blocks (3-4), claves (3-4), maracas (2-3), cabasa, guiros (2-3), 
temple blocks, vibraslap, ratchet, whip, castanets, piccolo blocks, 
rain sticks (2-3), log drums (2-3) 
Metal - tambourines (3-4), triangles (3-4), finger cymbals (2-3), cowbells (2-3), 
jingle bells (3-4), sleigh bells, agogo bells, flex-a-tone, suspended cymbal, 
wind chimes, bell tree, gong 
Skin - hand drums (10-12; eventually one per child), conga drums/tubanos (3-4), 
bongo drums, djembe, timpani, snare drum, bass drum 
There are additional unpitched instruments such as sound effects (train whistle, slide whistle, bird calls, 
thunder sheet, ocean drum, cricket call, washboard, spoons, etc.) and authentic ethnic instruments (kalimba, 
shekere, African pods, gankogui, Taos drum, etc.) that also may be added.

• large unpitched percussion grouped together
