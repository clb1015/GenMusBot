CM11+ Welcome to GAMEPLAN Grade Two. 
GAMEPLAN is an active music curriculum for children. Organized by grade level and 
presented chronologically in weekly lessons, GAMERAN sequences musical skills and 
objectives that support the National Standards for Arts Education. As good teaching is 
diverse, this curriculum is eclectic. It embraces several philosophies, including the 
principles of Orff Schulwerk and the Kodaly approach to reading rhythm and melody. 
Nursery rhymes, folk songs, and traditional children's games and dances form an integral 
part of the repertoire included. 
GAMEPLAN as two goals: 
• to organize musical objectives in a logical progression that is educationally 
sound and developmentally appropriate 
• to present the lessons in a concise format that is practical, easy to follow, and 
user-friendly 
GAMERAN parallels the school year from September through May and includes one 
lesson per week with approximately 60 minutes of activities. This is an active music 
curriculum. There are no student textbooks. All charts and visuals appear in the 
Appendices, and any additional materials required are listed on page x. 
The following pages define and outline grade two of this curriculum. Take time to 
familiarize yourself with this information, as it explains how and why the lessons unfold.

Second grade students are remarkably open-minded and display considerable flexibility. At 
this age they are curious and willing to persevere. As attention spans increase, lessons can 
include activities that last up to twenty minutes, but no longer! Like first grade students, 
they enjoy repetition and are now ready to work with partners and in small groups. 
GAMEPLAN includes one lesson per week with approximately 60 minutes of activities. 
Each of the lessons is planned sequentially, addressing objectives through a variety of 
experiences in different stages of learning. Many (ionsiderations were made when planning 
the lessons; however, most teachers do not instruct students once a week for an hour. 
Consequently, as you adapt the lessons to your own teaching circumstances, keep in mind 
the following: 
• multiple activities are needed because attention spans are short (if you see 
students more than once a week, consider introducing several activities 
during the first lesson and reviewing or extending them during the second) 
• if you instruct students less than 60 minutes per week, consider the stage of 
teaching for each activity (introduce, reinforce, assess) when determining 
which ones to include 
• most teachers will not have all of the instruments and materials needed to teach 
every lesson, but do not be discouraged . . . building a music program is an 
ongoing process and requires flexibility and a willingness to adapt 
In every lesson: 
• state the objective(s) at the beginning and review the concepts at the end 
• transition from activity to activity quickly 
• develop ways to "re-focus" the students during an activity; for example: 
stand/sit, reposition your placement in the room, introduce a visual, etc. 
▪ include movement . . . in every lesson 
• develop questioning techniques that require higher-level thinking skills 
• see the experience through the students' eyes. . . What is special? What will 
they enjoy? What will they share with their parents when they get home? 
Unless you are a beginning teacher, you probably have activities you have successfully 
experienced with your second grade students. If you would like to incorporate them into 
GAIVIERAN: 
• identify which objective(s) the activity best supports (be certain the teaching 
process teaches to the objective) 
• determine whether the activity best introduces, reinforces, or assesses the 
objective (this will help you know when to teach the activity) 
• consult the "Year At A Glance" chart on page viii to know when to substitute 
or add the activity 
NATIONAL STANDARDS • MUSIC 
The National Standards for Arts Education - Music Content Standards: 
1. Singing, alone and with others, a varied repertoire of music 
2. Performing on instruments, alone and with others, a varied repertoire of music 
3. Improvising melodies, variations, and accompaniments 
4. Composing and arranging music within specified guidelines 
5. Reading and notating music 
6. Listening to, analyzing, and describing music 
7. Evaluating music and music performances 
8. Understanding relationships between music, the other arts, and disciplines outside the arts 
9. Understanding music in relat ion to history and culture Children learn by doing. They need room to move and should be active participants in the music

Hand Drum Technique 
By February, second grade students are ready to learn proper hand drum 
technique. The purpose of this activity is to introduce how to hold and play 
the instrument. Although it would be ideal to have one per student, sharing 
drums works well. Ten and twelve inch drums are best for this age child; 
however, having a variety of sizes will afford a range of pitch as hand drum 
activities become more sophisticated (canons, two-part scores, etc.) 
Nontunable hand drums are less expensive than tunable hand drums, and as 
with other instruments, purchasing high quality is recommended. 
When introducing hand drum technique: 
• begin without drums, students standing facing teacher 
- model clapping one hand on the fleshy part of the opposite palm 
(under the thumb) 
- echo simple four-beat patterns 
• label the parts of the drum (head and rim) and model how to 
hold the drum: 
- hold rim with thumb on top, head of drum perpendicular to floor 
- support drum with elbow bent, held halfway between shoulder and waist 
- transfer clapping patterns to playing drum with fingertips on the edge 
of the drum closest to the body 
- allow all students to echo several simple four-beat patterns 
• give students the opportunity to explore other ways to produce 
sounds or sound effects on the drum 
- tap rim, scrape head

Mirroring 
There are many musical benefits to mirroring. Students experience different 
tempi, explore time/space/shape/force in movement, distinguish between 
right and left, and visualize the form in music. Begin without recorded music 
with the teacher leading (class facing teacher). Keep the movements slow 
and fluid, exploring levels and incorporating ways to bend, flex, twist, turn, 
push, pull, expand, collapse, and rotate. Encourage students to remain silent. 
Add recorded music, preferably instrumental, slow, and tranquil

Movement: 
Students form groups of five, four to represent the buggy with the fifth in the 
center ("Mary Jane"). The four students stand as two couples; one in front of 
the other. Couples join inside hands, joining outside hands with other 
couple to enclose "Mary Jane." During the A section buggies move through 
the space without bumping into one another. During the B section buggies 
stand still and "Mary Janes" find a new buggy. Game continues, AB 
repeated.

2 rounds. Select a student to choose a "magic word." The teacher as the leader

word" is heard again. Game continues.

Moving with a Prop 
The use of props is effective when students explore levels and shapes in 
movement. They take the focus away from the child and can provide a 
source for imagery. 
Every music room should have a set of scarves. Available in a variety of sizes 
and colors, look for fabric that is lightweight and billowy, such as chiffon. If 
scarves are not available, consider substituting crepe paper streamers. 
Process: - distribute scarves, one to each student in scattered formation 
- in personal space, students explore ways to move the scarves 
(raise, lower, sway, twirl, turn, flick, etc.) 
- given a sound cue (teacher plays accent on hand drum), 
students freeze into statues incorporating the scarves 
- adding locomotor movement, use imagery to explore new 
ideas such as floating like a cloud, drifting like 
a sailboat, spinning like a tornado, etc.

The most common pentatonic scales used in Orff Schulwerk are based on Do-Re-Mi-So-La; 
consequently there are no half steps. 
Each of the major pentatonic scales has a relative minor. The notes are the same, however 
the tonal center changes. Minor scales are based on La.

Few teachers begin a teaching assignment with adequate instruments and equipment. 
Furnishing a music room often requires the support of the administration and community. 
It can take several years. Fund raising, soliciting assistance from parent organizations, and 
applying for local or national grants should all be considered when equipping the music 
room. 
Use the information listed below as a guide. The size of the teaching space and the amount 
of students per class will impact the numbers. Ideally the music room should have a large 
open space for movement. Children do not mind sitting on the floor. If the average class 
size is between 25-30 students, the following is recommended: 
• pitched instruments 
Soprano Glockenspiels 3-4 
Alto Glockenspiels 3-4 
Soprano Metallophone 1-2 
Soprano Xylophones 2-3 
Alto Metallophones 1-2 
Alto Xylophones 3-4 
Bass Metallophone 1 
Bass Xylophones 2-3 
Contra Bass Bars: 
C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C 
• unpitched instruments 
Buy quality instruments. Begin with a bass and alto 
xylophone and one each of the glockenspiels. Add 
multiples and metallophones when able. Eventually 
consider six to eight glockenspiels, as they are less 
expensive and afford more students the opportunity to 
play. If you are unable to purchase all of the contra 
bass bars at one time, begin with the C-D-F-G and 
add the others later. Students will demonstrate better 
mallet technique if instruments are raised off of the 
floor. Stands are available and are helpful when 
instruments need to be moved. 
Wood - wood blocks (3-4), claves (3-4), maracas (2-3), cabasa, guiros (2-3), 
temple blocks, vibraslap, ratchet, whip, castanets, piccolo blocks, 
rain sticks (2-3), log drums (2-3) 
Metal - tambourines (3-4), triangles (3-4), finger cymbals (2-3), cowbells (2-3), 
jingle bells (3-4), sleigh bells, agogo bells, flex-a-tone, suspended cymbal, 
wind chimes, bell tree, gong 
Skin - hand drums (10-12; eventually one per child), conga drums/tubanos (3-4), 
bongo drums, djembe, timpani, snare drum, bass drum 
There are additional unpitchecl instruments such as sound effects (train whistle, slide whistle, bird calls, 
thunder sheet, ocean drum, cricket call, washboard, spoons, etc.) and authentic ethnic instruments (kalimba, 
shekere, African pods, gankogui, Taos drum, etc.) that also may be added.

When arranging the room, keep in mind the following: 
• traffic patterns as students enter and exit 
• pitched instruments grouped from low to high (players' perspective) 
• logical rotation pathway from instrument to instrument 
• large unpitched percussion grouped together 
• small unpitched percussion easily accessible (baskets, tubs, shelves, table) KID SOUNDS PUBUCA11ONS
