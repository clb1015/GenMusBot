GAMEPLAN is an active music curriculum for children. Organized by grade level and 
presented chronologically in weekly lessons, GAMERAN sequences musical skills and 
objectives that support the National Standards for Arts Education. As good teaching is 
diverse, this curriculum is eclectic. It embraces several philosophies, including the 
principles of Orff Schulwerk and the Kodaly approach to reading rhythm and melody. Folk 
songs, folk rhymes, and traditional games and dances form an integral part of the repertoire 
included. 
GAMERAN has two goals: 
• to organize musical objectives in a logical progression that is educationally 
sound and developmentally appropriate 
• to present the lessons in a concise format that is practical, easy to follow, and 
user-friendly 
GAMERAN parallels the school year from September through May and includes one 
lesson per week with approximately 60 minutes of activities. This is an active music 
curriculum. There are no student textbooks. All charts and visuals appear in the 
Appendices, and any additional materials required are listed on page x. Included in Grade 
Five are activities from folk dance collections and the first volume of Carl Orff and Gunild 
Keetman's Music for Children. 
Grade Five also includes Hot Tunes, a set of 22 reproducible pages for students (located 
inside the pocket of the back cover). Hot Tunes is to Grade Five what Cool Tunes was 
to Grade Four . . . a study of the soprano recorder with songs and activity pages integrated 
throughout the school year. Permission is granted to duplicate these pages annually for 
students in one school. These pages may not be shared with other teachers as this is an 
infringement of copyright. 
Two Appendices should be noted in this grade level . . . Appendix D: Recorder and 
Appendix E: Performing Groups. Each contains useful information. 
The following pages define and outline Grade Five of this curriculum. Take time to 
familiarize yourself with this information, as it explains how and why the lessons unfold. 
GAMEPLAN is the result of a combined total of over 50 years of teaching music to 
children. During that time many teachers, colleagues, friends, and family influenced our 
work; however, the greatest impact on this project came from our students. For all of the 
joy, fascination, frustration, and wonder they provide, GAMERAN is dedicated to those 
children . . . our greatest teachers.

The lessons in GAMERAN • GRADE FIVE presume the following: 
Objectives are categorized into five conceptual areas. Air= 
Rhythm - demonstrate pulse (quarter, half, dotted half, and whole note), recognize 
note values, identify time signatures (including 5/4 and 7/8), 
experience mixed meter, and demonstrate Question-Answer 
improvisation (body percussion/unpitched/barred instruments) 
Melody - demonstrate proper vocal technique, demonstrate proper playing technique 
on the soprano recorder, experience modal melodies, and continue to 
build repertoire 
Harmony - demonstrate proper mallet technique, explore canon/round, create 
ostinati, and analyze chord change accompaniments 
Form- refine simple forms and experience and label extended forms 
Timbre - continue to identify musical symbols, explore musical expression, and 
continue to identify orchestral families 
Students approach objectives through a variety of experiences. 
Move - explore locomotor and nonlocomotor responses in metered and 
non-metered settings and continue to develop a repertoire of 
organized dance 
Speak - use the voice rhythmically or freely to explore vocal expression 
Sing - match pitch independently and within a group 
Listen - develop aural recognition 
Play-explore the body as an instrument as well as pitched and unpitched instruments 
Notate - demonstrate an understanding of the visual representation of rhythm, 
melody, and form

the remainder of the year. Lessons in Grade Five continue to reinforce skills and objectives

Fifth grade students are independent and self-assured. They are prepared to withstand diverse 
circumstances. In the music classroom they are capable of more complex ensemble work 
and can make sophisticated contributions. The fifth grader possesses a certain goodness. . . 
an acceptance of things as they are. 
GAMERAN includes one lesson per week with approximately 60 minutes of activities. 
Each of the lessons is planned sequentially, addressing objectives through a variety of 
experiences in different stages of learning. Many considerations were made when planning 
the lessons; however, most teachers do not instruct students once a week for an hour. 
Consequently, as you adapt the lessons to your own teaching circumstances, keep in mind 
the following: 
• although attention spans have increased, fifth grade students still need a 
variety of activities in each lesson (if you see students more than once a 
week, consider introducing the activities during the first lesson and 
reviewing or extending them during the second) 
• if you instruct students less than 60 minutes per week, consider the stage of 
teaching for each activity (introduce, reinforce, assess) when determining 
which ones to include 
• most teachers will not have all of the instruments and materials needed to teach 
every lesson, but do not be discouraged . . . building a music program is an 
ongoing process and requires flexibility and a willingness to adapt 
In every lesson: 
• state the objective(s) at the beginning and review the concepts at the end 
• transition from activity to activity quickly 
• develop ways to "re-focus" the students during an activity; for example: 
stand/sit, reposition your placement in the room, introduce a visual, etc. 
• include movement. . . in every lesson 
• develop questioning techniques that require higher-level thinking skills 
• see the experience through the students' eyes . . . What is special? What will 
they enjoy? What will they share with their parents when they get home? 
Unless you are a beginning teacher, you probably have activities you have successfully 
experienced with your fifth grade students. If you would like to incorporate them into 
GAMERAN: 
• identify which objective(s) the activity best supports (be certain the teaching 
process teaches to the objective) 
• determine whether the activity best introduces, reinforces, or assesses the 
objective (this will help you know when to teach the activity) 
• consult the "Year At A Glance" chart on page viii to know when to substitute 
or add the activity

to have a few new students in each class. This lesson includes a brief overview of all recorder

Movement Game 
Introduced in fourth grade, this movement game challenges students to perform 
rhythmic ostinati and the underlying pulse simultaneously. 
In scattered formation students move to the quarter note pulse played by the 
teacher on the lower register of the piano (using black keys only). While 
moving to the pulse, students quietly clap the four-beat ostinato played by the 
teacher on the upper register of the piano (black keys only). Occasionally 
change the four-beat ostinato. 
Note: The piano improvisation may utilize clusters of pitches or 
individual black keys. 
Choose two students to improvise on the black keys of the piano, one to play 
the pulse on the lower register and the other to create a four-beat ostinato on 
the upper register. Class simultaneously responds in movement. Repeat with 
new volunteers. 
If time allows, encourage the student using the lower register of the piano to 
explore different pulses (half, whole, or eighth).

Conducting with Hula Hoops 
This conducting game is based on an idea by Wolfgang Hartmann and is 
shared with his permission. The activity allows students to explore and classify 
unpitched instruments. 
Formation: circle, seated (each student with an unpitched instrument) 
Process: - after choosing unpitched instruments, students sit in one 
circle, grouped by timbre (wood, metal, skin) 
- in center of circle, teacher spins one hula hoop (like a top) 
and students play instruments to reflect the movement 
of the hoop (explore tempo, dynamics, ritard, 
accelerando, etc.) 
- using three hula hoops, assign each a timbre (wood, metal, 
skin) and students react as teacher spins each 
hoop (individually or simultaneously)

Mirroring with Partners 
Students should be familiar with the technique of mirroring, having had several 
opportunities in grades two, three, and four. 
Quickly assign partners and determine who will lead first. Students begin by 
forming an interesting shape, and the first student leads until the teacher plays 
a sound cue (finger cymbals), transferring the lead. Encourage students to keep 
the movement fluid as the lead changes. Discourage any talking or noise. 
After both students have had the opportunity to lead, the teacher plays a 
different sound cue (suspended cymbal or gong), signaling a change of 
partners. Students move silently and fluidly to find a new partner. Without 
speaking, new partners establish who will lead first. The following recording 
is long enough to allow several combinations of partners and leaders.

Shadowing with Partners 
Introduced in GAMERAN Grade Four, shadowing as an extension to mirroring 
is exactly what it implies: the action duplicates the movement from behind, 
and the same sides of the body are used. 
Quickly assign partners and determine who will lead first. Both students face 
the same direction, the leader in front of the shadower. Students begin by 
forming an interesting shape, and the first student leads until the teacher plays 
a sound cue (finger cymbals) transferring the lead. When the lead is 
transferred, both students turn to face the opposite direction so that the new 
leader is in front of the shadower.

This activity explores expression in movement using the cards introduced in 
October, Week Two. To prepare the following game, begin with the class (in 
scattered formation) standing facing the teacher. One at a time, the teacher 
holds up one of the expression cards. In silence, each student interprets the 
expression in movement. Note: The students may choose to freeze into a 
shape or explore locomotor movement. 
Game: 
Divide the class into two teams. Team One chooses two or three students to 
guess the expression performed in movement by the remainder of the team. 
Teacher holds up a card not visible to the students guessing, and the team 
interprets the expression in movement. The students guessing may confer 
before giving their answer. If correct, the team receives one point. The game 
continues with Team Two. Note: Each time a team takes a turn, different 
students should be given the opportunity to guess.

Rhythm Cards 
This activity combines rhythmic reading with mallet technique. Assign students 
to barred instruments set in C pentatonic (remove F's and B's). One at a time, 
present each rhythm card; students speak the rhythm using rhythm syllables 
followed by playing the rhythm using any available pitches. Demonstrate how 
to move stepwise, sometimes repeating pitches, to create a more melodic 
improvisation. 
Note: If the number of barred instruments is limited, consider assigning 
students partners or choose a few instruments and seat students in short lines 
behind instruments. Students move to the end of the lines after each rhythm.

Choo Choo Ch'Boogie 
This activity gives individual students the opportunity to improvise a short 
movement sequence to be echoed by the class. 
With students in scattered formation, play the recording listed below. The 
leader (teacher at first) improvises a quick eight-beat phrase using body 
percussion, non locomotor, or locomotor movement and freezes while the class 
echoes the phrase. The lead can change at any time by calling a student's 
name or by having students raise hands to volunteer. 
Note: The recording begins with an unmetered train whistle followed by 
a 32-beat instrumental Introduction (four quick eight-beat phrases).

Choo Choo Ch'Boogie from Five Guys Named Moe

Overview 
Playing the recorder is a vital part of an active elementary music program. It is an opportunity 
for every student to own their own instrument and the benefits are numerous: 
• improves note reading skills and develops musicianship 
• provides an instrument for improvisation 
• challenges older students 
• improves ear training 
• provides an affordable, portable instrument for practice outside of the classroom 
and at home 
• transitions to band instruments as well as providing lifelong enjoyment 
• adds a wind instrument to the Orff instrumentarium 
• develops ensemble performance 
Although GAMERAN includes recorder materials in Grades Four and Five, the student 
materials make no mention of a specific grade level, providing flexibility to teachers choosing 
to begin at a different time. 
Getting Started 
There are several decisions to make before introducing the recorder. Although individual 
teaching circumstances will impact these decisions, the following is recommended: 
• All students own a recorder. 
• All students receive a packet of music. 
• The school owns a class set of recorders. 
Before introducing the recorder: 
• Purchase a class set of Baroque soprano recorders for the school and label them 
with a permanent marker or an engraving tool. Many high-quality plastic 
recorders are available at a reasonable cost. 
• Prepare a letter to be sent home with each student. See sample on page 152. 
Have the administrator approve the letter before it is duplicated. 
• Establish a system for selling recorders (before/after school, through the office, 
during music class, etc.), and order recorders in advance. 
• Duplicate the Hot Tunes music packet. It is recommended students receive 
the packet with the recorder. Adjust the price of the recorder/packet 
if necessary to cover duplicating costs. Sample letter to parents: Sample Letter

General Teaching Tips 
Establish class routines: 
• when students may play 
• recorder "resting" positions 
• consequences for playing out of turn 
Model correct playing technique: 
• hand position (left hand on top) 
• posture (recorder pointed down) 
• finger position (cover holes with pads of fingers) 
• breathing (slow, warm air) 
• tonguing (icluh" for lower notes, "tu" for higher register) 
Trouble shooting: 
Common problems 
• recorder squeaks 
• over blowing 
• incorrect pitches 
• recorder "hums" 
Solutions 
• use less air 
• use warm air (imagery of "fogging up window") 
• cover holes tightly 
• remind student not to speak or sing

Overview 
Most elementary music programs include at least one performing group, and the possibilities 
are many. Although the most obvious is an elementary choir, ensembles may also be 
instrumental or movement based. Regardless of the type of ensemble, it is wise to wait until 
students are in the upper grades before providing them this opportunity. Potentially large 
numbers, limited rehearsal time, and the sophistication of performance repertoire make it 
more appropriate for older students. 
Consider including both fourth and fifth grade students in ensembles, as this will build the 
program from year to year, and the older students can model musicianship and desired 
behaviors. 
Often the new music specialist inherits an existing performance group. If this is not the case, 
the teacher may choose from a variety of options, such as: 
Choir 
Percussion Ensemble 
Recorder Ensemble 
Drum Ensemble 
Folk Dance Group 
Auditions 
A difficult but important decision . . . Is the performing group auditioned or open to every 
student? Although no student should be turned away, the most musically satisfying 
experiences will be generated from students that meet certain criteria. One possible solution 
is to have more than one ensemble. For example, students that don't match pitch may be 
an asset to an instrumental group, and a student that has difficulty functioning in a large 
ensemble may be better placed in a smaller group. 
Even if all students are welcome to participate, it is good for them to experience the audition 
process. Choose and announce a specific time/s (before/after school, lunch recess or during 
class), and have students complete a short survey listing name, grade, classroom teacher, 
and which performing group is of interest. Consider the following suggestions: 
Choir auditions 
• teach all students part of a new song (with a range of at least an octave) 
• listen to students in small groups or duets and then individually 
Instrument auditions 
• echo clap eight-beat phrases with the whole group and then individually 
• teach a short pattern to be played on barred instruments, drums, or recorder; 
students play in small groups and then individually 
Movement auditions 
• echo eight-beat phrases with four levels of body percussion first with the 
whole group and then individually 
• teach a short movement sequence (include specific right/left and balance 
challenges); students perform in small groups Few teachers begin a teaching assignment with adequate instruments and equipment. 
Furnishing a music room often requires the support of the administration and community. 
It can take several years. Fund raising, soliciting assistance from parent organizations, and 
applying for local or national grants should all be considered when equipping the music 
room 
Use the information listed below as a guide. The size of the teaching space and the amount 
of students per class will impact the numbers. Ideally the music room should have a large 
open space for movement. Children do not mind sitting on the floor. If the average class 
size is between 25-30 students, the following is recommended: 
• pitched instruments 
Soprano Glockenspiels 3-4 
Alto Glockenspiels 3-4

Buy quality instruments. Begin with a bass and alto 
xylophone and one each of the glockenspiels. Add 
multiples and metallophones when able. Eventually 
consider six to eight glockenspiels, as they are less 
expensive and afford more students the opportunity to 
play. If you are unable to purchase all of the contra 
bass bars at one time, begin with the C-D-F-G and 
add the others later. Students will demonstrate better 
mallet technique if instruments are raised off of the 
floor. Stands are available and are helpful when 
instruments need to be moved. 
• unpitched instruments 
Wood - wood blocks (3-4), claves (3-4), maracas (2-3), cabasa, guiros (2-3), 
temple blocks, vibraslap, ratchet, whip, castanets, piccolo blocks, 
rain sticks (2-3), log drums (2-3) 
Metal - tambourines (3-4), triangles (3-4), finger cymbals (2-3), cowbells (2-3),

wind chimes, bell tree, gong 
Skin -hand drums (10-12; eventually one per child), conga drums/tubanos (3-4), 
bongo drums, djembe, timpani, snare drum, bass drum 
There are additional unpitched instruments such as sound effects (train whistle, slide whistle, bird calls, 
thunder sheet, ocean drum, cricket call, washboard, spoons, etc.) and authentic ethnic instruments (kalimba, 
shekere, African pods, gankogui, Taos drum, etc.) that also may be added.

yarn balls, tennis balls, bean bags, scarves, puppets

is granted to duplicate these pages annually for the 
students in one school. 
To provide flexibility, no page numbers are included and 
there is no reference to fifth grade. If duplicating the 
entire packet, the order below shows the chronological 
sequence.
