INTRODUCTION
During the past three years a countless number of educators, music teachers
and parents have enquired about the work I am doing with young children at
The Royal Conservatory of Music in Toronto. Most of these people have heard
about Carl Orff and his “Music for Children” but they want more detailed information. The questions are almost invariably the same — ‘How do Orft’s
ideas differ from those of other existing methods:’ and, ‘How does the teacher
develop these ideas:’. In the following pages we will discuss Carl Orff and the
expansion of his doctrine that music is the natural outcome of speech, rhythm
and movement.
At sixty-four years of age (b. 1895) Carl Orff enjoys an international reputation as one of Germany’s greatest present-day composers. Works such as
Carmina Burana, Catulli Carmina, Der Mond, Die Kluge and Antigonae were
originally the keystone of his fame. But today this renown is shared by another
facet of his ability, the role of music educator, and the creator of music which
is essentially for performance by young children.
Since 1926 Orff has been closely associated with youngsters through his work
with the Guentherschule — a school for gymnastics, dance and music in his
native city of Munich. Then in 1948 he did a series of radio broadcasts with
children over the Bayerischer Rundfunk. The experience of these years and
the collected material were condensed into five volumes, known throughout
Europe and other countries as ‘Das Schulwerk’ and in English-speaking countries as ‘Music for Children’.
The life of the average family today is not conducive to music-making within
the home. In many cases the child is given music lessons as a matter of course,
just as he receives vitamin pills —Orff has long felt that educationally speaking
we are putting the cart before the horse; that music is approached as an intellectual process which begins with the introduction of the staff, the treble clef,
middle C and the mathematical division of bars. Compare this ‘with our school
system where it is a prerequisite that the child be able to speak and to communicate freely with the world around him before he learns the alphabet and to
read and write. Only when we make this comparison do we realize that the
youngster is not ready to make music. He is like a child who has lived withdeaf mutes and who has never experienced the joys of expressing himself in
song, and in the rhythmic jargon of children at play.
Creative art will always be a battleground for clashing viewpoints but it is
this conflict which strengthens and refines and from which the best emerges.
Music educators are becoming increasingly aware of the need for musical participation BEFORE the child approaches serious study on a chosen instrument
and as a result many methods have come into existence. But it seems that the
most valuable and musically rewarding is one which requires that the child
plays an essential role in the creation of ensemble music. It is here that we recognize the superiority of Carl Orff’s ‘Music for Children.’
He is constantly aware ofthe vital force that rhythm represents and whether
we listen to his ‘Carmina Burana’ or a simple children’s song, it is clear that
rhythm is the predominant feature of his work. As music teachers we too often
think of rhythm as a mathematical problem, rather than an entity which forms
the axis of all living forms. Our bodies function with this life force in the unconscious rhythm of our hearts, our breathing, our walking; in nature we have the
seasons, the rising and setting of the sun, the continuity of day and night and
the phases ofthe moon. Let us accept Orff’s credo that rhythm is expressed constantly in the speech and movements of every child and that we must develop
it through these mediums.
Long before the child sees the printed page of notes he should recognize that
music consists of grouped tones of different strength, and that there are two
basic arrangements, the strong beat followed by the weak beat, as in the march,
or the strong beat followed by two weak ones, as in the waltz. Many parents
and teachers feel that the rhythm band adequately performs this function. Fundamentally most methods of pre-instrumental training cope with the problem
of duple and triple rhythm varying only in the manner of procedure. But within
the ranks of present day music educators Carl Orff isthe only one who develops
rhythmic perception through its natural evolution of speech, rhythm and
movement —three prehistoric elements inmusic.
Orff uses the natural meter of words and the child’s instinctive desire to incorporate speech, rhythm and movement as the basis of developing rhythmic
independence. At first he learns to distinguish between two and three pulse
meters by using the names of flowers, trees, birds, etc., the syllables of which
are pronounced first in two beats, then in three. For example:

Mallet-played Instruments
To encourage and facilitate ensemble playing Carl Orff has designed a complete set of instruments especially adapted to the physical requirements of children. In construction and tonal quality the instruments are similar to the chimes
and gambangs of a Javanese orchestra, and allow many possibilities for contrast
of tone and colour according to the combination ofthe instruments. Equipped
with metal bars, the glockenspiels and metalophones have aclear, ringing tone
quite unlike the dull, hollow sound of the hardwood xylophone bars. All are
tuned in the key of C major.

Mallet Technique
The quality of tone one produces from these instruments is affected not
only by the movement of the arms and wrist, but also by the type of mallets
used and the manner in which they are held.
Generally speaking, the xylophones and metalophones are played with feltheaded mallets, while those used for the glockenspiels are made of wood with
a small insert of ebony on the tip. For special effects wooden mallets may be
used for the xylophones causing a brittle and decisive tone, and the brilliance
of the glockenspiels may be subdued by using rubber-headed mallets.
Regardless of which mallets are used, the technique for holding them is the
same. Grasp the stick between the thumb and first and second fingers so that it
lies in the middle of the second joint and allows freedom of the wrist. Do not
let the index finger rest in a pointed position along the stick; this cramps the
hand and wrist and in many cases is responsible for a dull hard tone.
When movements are tense, much preparatory work should be done with
patschen and clapping (see chapter III) so that the children feel relaxed and are
able to transfer this to the instruments. To insure a resounding, clear tone the
notes should be struck in the center with a quick rebound of the stick, wrist
and hand.
Musical Glasses
There are many occasions when glockenspiels can be augmented by the use
of musical glasses. Tumblers orstemmed-ware may be used but each one should
have a clear, bell-like tone when struck by a wooden mallet. Much time and
care should be given to the selection of glasses and, with luck, you may find
some that have a definite pitch and require no tuning. This is particularly true
if you use only two or three notes such as G, Eand A. When using the complete
pentatonic scale you may have to tune one or two of the glasses by adding water
to lower the pitch. The best tone can be produced by holding a glockenspiel
mallet loosely in the hand and striking the edge of the glass with a quick,
elastic movement.String Instruments
One of the most important items is a deep-toned string instrument to give
body and support to the ensemble. Throughout Book I you will find reference
to the gamba or lute and, in many cases, simply bass. The guitar or cello may
be substituted for any one of these aslong as the instrument istuned to the basic
bordun C, G, C, G. Legato passages demanding along, sustained tone can be
played only on the gamba and cello and require facile bowing technique.
Recorder
It is essential that the teacher be able to play and improvise freely on the recorder whether the children are doing rhythmic body movements or basic
ostinati on the instruments. The soft, reedy tone blends well with the whole
ensemble and has a unique effect when used as a solo instrument.
Triangle
The triangle is a round steel bar bent into a three-cornered shape and held by
a loop of cord or catgut. It is struck by a beater of the same material and should
be kept away from the body with the arms in a free position. Triangles come
in different sizes, each with a characteristic sound and of a penetrating tone
quality which is more effective when the instrument is used sparingly.
Cymbals
Cymbals are two convex brass plates.of equal size, with aleather strap attached
through a hole in the middle of each one. Acymbal holder can be used for one
cymbal or the strap may be held in the left hand while striking the rim with a
stick or felt-headed mallet. This isthe best procedure to follow when the cymbal
is used as an accompanying figure. If a strong rhythmic accent or a climax is
desired grasp the straps firmly in each hand and clash the cymbals together with
a vertical movement. Antique cymbals are smaller, made of thicker metal and
are more accurately tuned.
Wood Block
No detailed description is necessary for this rhythm instrument, the name of
which is self-explanatory. It can be held in the left hand or placed on a solid
base of similar material so that the sound is not deadened. Use a wooden, felt
or rubber-headed mallet according to the quality of tone desired.
Jingles or Shells
Jingles orshells are small sleigh bells attached to a leather band which can be
held in the hand or fastened around the ankle. They are used for a light, rhythmic accompaniment.
10Rattles
Rattles are easily manufactured by filling wooden or metal boxes with sand,
buckshot, etc., and the children enjoy making their own.
Tambourine
The tambourine is a small, single-headed drum with jingles inserted at regular
intervals in the shell. Hold it in the left hand with the thumb extended along
the shell and the fingers curled underneath.
For a simple rhythmical figure strike the head on the edge with the outstretched fingers ofthe right hand. The instrument should be held away from
the body and played with the arms in a free position. A roll is performed by
grasping the shell firmly in the right hand and shaking it. If a strong accent is
desired at the end of the roll bring the instrument down and strike the head
against the knee.

Bass Drum
This is a large drum with a deep, heavy tone. It has two heads, can be tuned
to a definite pitch and is played with a large soft-headed stick.

Physical response to music is inherent in all people and we must learn to use
this to full advantage in our work with children. Rhythmic perception can be
heightened and accentuated by simple body movements which do not require
delicate and precise co-ordination. But whether these consist of clapping, patschen (slapping the knees or thighs with the palm of the hands), stamping,
finger snapping, or combinations of all four, the movements must be free and
unhindered.
Clapping
Stand with the body erect, elbows out, arms relaxed and away from the body.
Turn the left palm upwards and use the right hand to clap. As the right arm is
lifted the wrist raises accordingly until the hand and arm resemble the neck of
a swan. As the right arm descends the wrist joint lowers accordingly. The left
hand and arm remain stationary throughout.
Patschen
Patschen, which can be performed either sitting orstanding, begins with the
arms in mid-air, elbows away from the body, wrists raised and hands extended
palm downwards over the knees. With a free movement arms descend, wrists
lower and hands slap the knees. Return to the original position with wrists
raising accordingly. Clapping and patschen, these movements may be combined.
Stamping
Stand with the body erect, weight on the left leg. Bend the right leg at the
knee and lift until the lower portion of the leg extends behind the knee. Return
it to position on the floor beside the left foot with a quick, sliding stamp.
Finger Snapping
Press the thumb and middle finger together then slide thumb away from the
finger with a quick, snapping movement.

THE DEVELOPMENT OF RHYTHMIC
AND MELODIC OSTINATI FROM SPEECH
Many children experience difficulty in feeling rhythm and the long line of
a phrase. Music teachers are constantly facing this problem and some of them
have evolved ways and means of solving the difficulty so that children progress
as quickly as possible to that pleasurable stage where they begin tomake music.
If rhythm is such an elemental force why does it present a problem to the
music teacher? Obviously the answer lies in the method of presentation. We
must throw away the apples and potatoes which are often used as a visual aid
for the mathematical understanding of the subdivision of the bar. We must
treat rhythm as an essence which is part of our living and breathing. We must
see that the child experiences music and creates music before he is told how it is
put together.
Let us begin with speech patterns, add simple movements such as clapping,
patschen, or combinations of these movements. Transfer the rhythms and
movements to melodic patterns on the instruments and at once we are making
primitive ensemble music. This aspect of musical development is invaluable
because the child learns from the beginning to relate his rhythmic and melodic
pattern to those of the other members of the group. In this way he absorbs and
feels relationship ofrhythm and melody. When he is completely at home with
the medium and is capable of expressing himself, musical notation is finally
introduced.

the easiest way is through speech, beginning with quarter notes since they are

which rhythm and melody evolve. The canon, a polyphonic composition of
simple imitation, isno exception to this. Uncomplicated bymelody or harmony
the children readily grasp the basic idea through speech canons as found on

and the precise duration of note values or rests.

THE RHYTHMIC AND MELODIC RONDO
In the first lessons preparation exercises are intentionally limited so that the
children relax and enjoy music-making. But the underlying purpose should not
be forgotten and as in any other artistic activity a solid foundation must be laid.
When properly instigated exercises can be given which will not only be a
source of pleasure but which will serve as a preparation for improvisation and
invention.
Since the technique of the instruments can be acquired only over a period
of time it is better to begin to extemporize with the instrument which the child
already commands —his body. Rhythmic exercises which use a variety of combinations of clapping, stamping, patschen and finger snapping are invaluable
aids towards developing freedom of rhythmic expression and all exercises
should lead to the goal of rhythmic and melodic improvisation.
Echo Clapping
Examples of echo clapping are given on page 80 of Book I. Begin with one
bar at a time if the children do not have an acute rhythmical sense and gradually increase this until they can reproduce a two bar and four bar phrase. In the
early lessons use only quarter and eight notes beginning on the first beat of the
bar, then add the up beat, dotted quarters, half notes etc. according to the ability of the children.
Rhythmic Phrase Building
This is the first step towards improvisation. Examples are given on page 84
of Book I. The children should be very comfortable with echo clapping before
being encouraged to make their own rhythms. The transition from imitation
to invention will be smoother when the teacher explains that each individual
will have the opportunity of completing a two bar phrase which will be improvised for every child. Extend the length of the phrases corresponding to the
progress of each person.
24Rhythmic Improvisation
This grows naturally out of phrase building and clapping, stamping, finger
snapping and patschen should be used as added interest.
Rhythmic Rondo
The rhythmic rondo, page 86, can be introduced very easily after the children
have become adept at echo clapping, phrase building and rhythmic improvisation. Consisting of a recurring refrain, or A section, with episodes in between
this instrumental form lends itself admirably to our purpose since the A section
can be established and performed by the whole group and the episode improvised by the individual.

Echo Playing
The sequence which leads towards melodic improvisation is closely allied
with the rhythmical development. Just as echo clapping is used to develop
rhythmic independence soecho playing becomes the first step towards melodic
freedom. Have the children remove the notes C, D and A from the instruments
and tell them that they will be using only the notes G and E. Discuss the sound
of these notes, which is high and which is low, and then play one or two bars
for each child.
The skill of the individual can be quickly assessed and the other notes may
be added as soon as the child is ready; A would be next and finally C and D.
Melodic Phrase Building
The ideas expressed in rhythmic phrase building are also applicable here and
once again the work is done with the individual. The teacher will play two
bars and ask a child to complete the phrase with a two bar pattern. The same
sequence of notes will be used as in echo playing and the.phrases may be extended to four, eight and sixteen bars, see page 85, Book I.

26CHAPTER VIII
LESSON PLANNING
There are two ways of preparing a lesson:
(1) Either work towards one goal, such as learning a complete piece, or:
(2) Use variety so that rhythmic, melodic and speech exercises progress step
by step towards alogical conclusion. When following the latter plan the teacher
should spend only four or five lessons on one theme to prevent boredom.
Before entering the classroom have the lesson clearly defined in your mind
know what you want to accomplish and how much you want to accomplish
in the ensuing period. At the same time be flexible and be ready to turn to
advantage any creative ideas advanced by the children. This will in turn make
the lessons more stimulating and spontaneous for both the teacher and the
pupils,
The first step in the preparation of a lesson is to choose the material you want
to use and then to plant it from a rhythmic and melodic point of view, and the
muscular co-ordination required for execution on the instruments. Remember
that if the movements of the children are jerky and uncontrolled, the same will
be true of the sound which they will produce from the instruments. Because
of this much time must be spent on the preparation of simple movements such
as clapping, and patschen. Muscular exercises should be evolved by the teacher
to overcome any apparent difficulties.
The first Lessons
The first lessons will obviously require much rhythmic activity including
clapping, patschen and stamping. Don’t try to do too much, instead aim for
quality so that the children feel they have accomplished something at the end
of each lesson.

30CHAPTER IX
GIVING A DEMONSTRATION
Sooner or later all parents want to see and hear what their child has accomplished, and a demonstration seemst to be the logical solution.
The easiest to prepare and the most stimulating musically, isthat demonstration for which approximately fifteen children have been chosen. From experience I have found that this number provides good choral and ensemble work
and eliminates the confusion caused by too many children on the platform.
Ideal as it may sound it is not always a good policy to choose only the best
performers as ‘Music for Children’ is designed for ALL children and most
educators want to see how each type fits in.
There is no danger of a child suddenly becoming self-conscious and stage
struck if he is made to realize in every lesson that the teacher, and only the
teacher, is the most important person in the room. When he is transferred to a
platform this feeling will persist and there will be no discipline problem or
lack of attention.
Restrict the demonstration to one hour, remember it is always better to leave
the audience wishing for more than groaning about the length of the programme to which they have been subjected.
When planning a program one should include all of the rhythmical and
melodic aspects of ‘Music for Children’.
